## One brand. Dozens of locations. Plenty of moving parts.

A franchise website sounds simple until every location needs its own hours, prices, events, menus, and staff access. Add a company-wide rebrand and a dated, difficult-to-manage website, and you've got a project I was very happy to get my hands on.

## A network, not 25 unrelated websites

Putt-Putt Fun Center operates mini-golf, arcades, and go-karts at independently run locations across the country. The original network launched with more than 30 locations; 24 franchise sites remain active today.

I built the replacement on **WordPress multisite**, with a corporate site and a separate web address for each franchise, such as `puttputt.com/lynchburg-va`. Shared location details live in the main site and are distributed with Broadcast. Corporate doesn't have to update the same information two dozen times, and visitors still get a site that feels local.

Permissions matter, too. Franchise staff can edit their own location without accidentally redecorating someone else's. If a location closes, I deactivate its site and redirect visitors to the locations directory rather than deleting everything. Because sometimes the next chapter is a reopening.

## The details that make the difference

Reusable components keep the network consistent, while Elementor handles page-specific layouts.

- **Hours:** Regular and holiday schedules with an open/closed indicator that updates when hours change
- **Pricing:** A consistent layout with location-specific details
- **Events:** Calendars for individual locations
- **Menus:** Location-specific food listings managed through Pods
- **Photos and specials:** Galleries and promotional slideshows

The goal wasn't to make every park identical. It was to give each one flexibility without turning the brand into 24 different design experiments.

## Handing over the controls

I created training sessions and video tutorials so franchise teams could manage hours, prices, events, menus, and images without disturbing the shared structure. A well-built system shouldn't require a developer every time someone changes the price of a game of mini-golf.

## The 2026 audit: getting the network back on course

I audited all 24 franchise sites and the corporate site, then built a client-facing dashboard showing scores, fixes, and checklists for each location.

- **25 of 25 sites** earned an A on the internal health checklist, scoring **92–100** with a **95 average**
- **127 fixes** completed across the network
- Hours and permissions reviewed and corrected
- Play and Party pages modernized with container layouts and a sticky header
- Contact, Our Story, Pricing, Events, and Menu pages aligned across locations
- Brand colors standardized throughout
- **79.8 hours** used against an **80-hour estimate**. Yes, I checked the math.

> The health grade comes from my project-specific audit checklist. It is **not** a Lighthouse score or a formal accessibility certification.

## Accessible across all 25 sites

Accessibility was part of the design work here too, and part of every audit I ran. Families of every age and ability book parties and look up hours on these sites, so I checked each location for the basics: heading structure, alt text, contrast, and keyboard-friendly navigation. Because it's one network, a fix made once reaches every location. Good accessibility ratings were a priority, and they support search visibility as well.
