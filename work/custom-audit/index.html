The problem

I look after about 120 WordPress websites for VistaMedia, and I manage sites outside of those as well. I needed a fast, repeatable way to see which sites may need assistance to hit better performance standards that wasn't solely reliant on Lighthouse. I also wanted plain language explanations that non-technical people could understand.

Lighthouse is excellent for what it does, but it tests one page and hands back separate scores meant for developers. Client-facing audit services gave me a grade, but I couldn’t control what they measured, or I was paying for a service I could run myself. I needed something I could control, and customize to each site's unique needs.

Where it started

Putt-Putt was the start of it. The audit work for the Putt-Putt Fun Center network was the first version of this tool from there it has grown and been tweaked to produce reports that are both actionable and easy to understand.

What I built

Second Look Site Check is a Node.js tool that reviews a site’s public pages and produces a branded, plain-language report with a single letter grade from A+ to F. It runs on my own computer, needs no logins to the site, and uses only what any visitor or search engine can see; meaning we could run it as a sales tool in addition to an internal actionable report.

Why it’s more than a Lighthouse score

I use Lighthouse for what it does best, accessibility and speed, then add everything it doesn’t look at.

  • Beyond the homepage It checks the homepage and up to 25 pages sampled across the sitemap, so one good page can’t hide a bad site.
  • Steadier speed results It runs the speed test three times after a warm-up and grades the median, so one unlucky run doesn’t decide the score.
  • Real visitors, not just a lab Where Google has real-visitor data for a site, it counts for half of the Speed score. A site can score badly in the lab and still be fast for the people using it, and the report shows both.
  • AI search It checks that the homepage has readable text and that tools like ChatGPT, Claude and Perplexity are allowed to read it.
  • The basics behind the site It reviews structured business details, the sitemap, hosting and caching, and whether the domain is protected against email spoofing.
  • Progress over time Every run is saved, and each report shows how the site has changed since its first audit.

How I keep it honest

  • The grade isn't about making a site look good A run weighs the actual impact of each issue on the site's performance and user experience, not just its appearance.
  • Rules are versioned Each result records which scoring rules it used, so I never compare a change in the rules to a change in the site.
  • Accessibility is described accurately WCAG is not a one-size-fits-all standard, and the report makes that clear. The report explains where the accessibility gaps are and how to fix them in plain language.
  • The wording is part of the product Reports say “search description” and “preferred address,” never tag names or crawler jargon. The backend reporting keeps the necessary technical details for reference as needed when remediation is requested.
  • White Label? Yup I'm building out a version that is allowing my contracted clients like VistaMedia an opportunity to utilize this as a white-labeled service for their clients.

How I use it

I audit a site, fix what the report ranks highest, audit again, and mark the site as improved. The before-and-after grades show the result without needing a conversation about technical details.

The proof

Outer Banks Coastal Life improved the most, from a D (65.1) to an A (94.4), a gain of 29.3 points. Virginia Beach Guide was next, from a D+ (68.8) to an A (95.6), up 26.8. Most of the gain came from Search, the largest part of the grade, which rose by 25 to 37 points on every site below.

Under the hood

Node.js, Lighthouse, Chrome, Puppeteer, and the Google Chrome UX Report API. Reports are generated as static HTML with print styles and exported to PDF.
