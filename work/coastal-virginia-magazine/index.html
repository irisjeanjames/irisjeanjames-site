Twenty-six years of stories. A whole new chapter.

The magazine began as HR Monthly, and became Coastal Virginia Magazine in 2000. It turned 26 in 2026.

A magazine website has to do more than look good. It needs to publish on schedule, support advertising, help readers find stories, and give the editorial team a system they can actually work with. Preferably without calling a developer every time an issue goes live.

Moving the archives without losing the plot

I helped migrate the magazine’s archive, with articles going back to 2010, from TownNews to WordPress. That’s a lot of stories, images, and history to bring along for the ride. While I was a team member when the migration happened, I later moved the site off its original prebuilt theme and onto a custom one built for Coastal Virginia, which gave the publication a more flexible foundation.

The issue isn’t finished until it’s published

The website had to work with the rhythm of a real magazine, not the other way around. Understanding the needs of the people making the publication is ultimately what helped me build an easy-to-use, well documented portal where the editorial calendar and deadlines were always in sync with the magazine’s schedule. I built the templates and a digital bookcase templates so each of the six yearly issues easy to find and read online, then helped make sure those issues actually went live on schedule set by the editors and staff.

Advertising pays the bills. Details matter.

I set up AdRotate Pro to manage advertising placements, so the team can quickly and readily add or remove ads as needed. An ad in the wrong place isn’t just a design issue. It’s a business issue. Reporting on ad performance allows advertisers to make informed decisions about their marketing spend, and helps the staff optimize ad placements for better results.

One publication, a family of brands

The magazine’s annual regional awards programs are run by a third party. While voting is handled through a 3rd party, the site hosts the contest results. Users needs a single place to access the businesses they supported and building the business directory (opens in a new tab) allowed for more opportunities for cross promotion within the publication family. The directory is a searchable, mapped directory with Best Of listings from 2019 through 2026, alongside the Be Well, Bridal, Dining, Eastern Shore, Home & Garden, Real Estate, and School / Education guides.

Coastal Virginia Magazine also hosts their annual CoVa Wine Fest (opens in a new tab), CoVa Burger Battle (opens in a new tab), and Coastal Virginia Idea House (opens in a new tab). I brought the magazine’s branding into the header of each related site so they feel like part of the same family, without pretending they’re all the same project.

CoVa Business, rebuilt

CoVa Business is the magazine’s sister publication. In 2026 I rebuilt its website (opens in a new tab), replacing an old, tangled theme and a stack of outdated plugins with a custom one. In the October 2026 audit it scored 91 (A-) overall as a starting point.

The 2026 audit: a little less waiting, a lot more accessibility

A lot of opportunity for growth throughout my career has come from learning opportunities. I didn't know when I rebuilt Coastal Virginia Magazine's website what accessibility, optimization, or other best practices entailed from a coding stand point. After re-developing the site, and then learning so much more about web development, I was anxious to apply everything I had learned through real world lessons when the October 2026 audit came around.

With more than 12,000 images uploaded by various users and no checks for proper alt text, and an initial C+ grade, the site needed work. One particularly unhelpful discovery: page caching was turned off, so pages were being rebuilt for every visit, with about 4.9 seconds before loading began. I enabled caching and fixed the structure behind accessibility: page headings, a marked main-content area for screen readers, article links that say where they lead, and teal text that needed stronger contrast.

Then came the image library. So many images needing alt text is not a job anyone could do by hand. I was able to tap into modern technology, and utilized an API call to Claude to review the images and draft the descriptions, reviewed what it produced, and wrote 12,035 of them into the database. This was a completely new project for me to take on, and I was thrilled to rise to the challenge. The outcome of the work to learn how to write code to automate this process wound up being my favorite part of this project.

To keep the gap from reappearing, I added a rule that builds alt text from clearly named new uploads, so the library stays described as it grows.
